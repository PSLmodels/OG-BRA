# Calibration of Government Parameters

## Government Spending as a Share of GDP

We've calibrated the vector $\alpha_G=[0.118]$ from IMF Government Finance Statistics and $\alpha_T=[0.038]$ from the same source.

### Government spending on infrastructure as a share of GDP

We set $\alpha_I = 0.02$: general-government public investment in Brazil averaged about 2% of GDP over 1995–2015 and remains near that level ([IMF PIMA Brazil](https://infrastructuregovern.imf.org/content/PIMA/Home/Region-and-Country-Information/Countries/Brazil.html); the 2023 fiscal framework also sets a federal investment floor of 0.6% of GDP). Note that the IMF GFS "expense" aggregate used for $\alpha_G$ excludes investment in nonfinancial assets, so $\alpha_I$ is not double-counted in $\alpha_G$.

The stock side is calibrated jointly with the flow. The same IMF source puts Brazil's general government capital stock at 35% of GDP in 2015 (against an emerging-market average of 92%), so `initial_Kg_ratio = 0.35`. In the model the steady-state public capital ratio is pinned by the identity $K_g/Y = \alpha_I/(g + \delta_g)$ with trend growth $g \approx 1.0\%$; matching the observed 35% stock with 2% investment requires `delta_g_annual = 0.047` rather than the ogcore default of 2% (the implied 4.7% depreciation is in line with the IMF capital-stock dataset's assumptions for emerging markets, and the identity is enforced by `tests/test_public_capital.py`). Without this adjustment the model would build public capital toward an artificial steady state of 66% of GDP, twice the measured stock.

Public capital enters production with share `gamma_g = 0.05`, carved out of the total capital share (private `gamma` falls from 0.407 to 0.357), following the convention used in OG-PHL. This is the conservative end of the meta-analysis range for the output elasticity of public capital (about 0.08–0.12 in Bom and Ligthart, 2014), which is appropriate here: with the low Brazilian $K_g/Y$ of 0.35, `gamma_g = 0.05` already implies a marginal product of public capital of about 14% per year, and higher elasticities would imply implausibly large returns.

## Distribution of Transfers Across Households

Aggregate transfers (`alpha_T`, 3.8% of GDP) are distributed across households by the matrix `eta`. The default `eta` distributed transfers in proportion to population, which misses the defining feature of Brazilian social transfers: targeting. The main non-pension programs in 2024 were Bolsa Família (about R\$168bn), the BPC assistance benefit (R\$112bn), and unemployment insurance plus the abono salarial (R\$106bn together). The World Bank reports that 73% of Bolsa Família reaches the poorest quintile and 94% the poorest two quintiles ([Second Bolsa Família Project](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/578141568314136393/pdf/Brazil-Second-Bolsa-Familia-Project.pdf)); BPC is somewhat less targeted (concentration coefficient −0.45 vs. −0.51 for Bolsa Família, [CEQ Working Paper 130](https://commitmentoequity.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/ceq130.pdf)); unemployment insurance and the abono go to formal workers and are only mildly progressive.

For unemployment insurance and the abono, we fit the quintile shares (3, 24, 46, 19, 8)% to two moments reported by IPEA's study of abono coverage ([Relatório de Pesquisa](https://repositorio.ipea.gov.br/server/api/core/bitstreams/7c99c033-0f15-4679-bfc5-9046f8d5b6dc/content), using PNAD 2013): only 15% of eligible workers are in the poorest 30% of household per-capita income, and about half are above the median — the mid-distribution hump typical of formal-worker programs. We blend the three program distributions with their expenditure weights, giving quintile shares of (49, 23, 18, 8, 3)% from poorest to richest, and map these to the model's seven lifetime-income groups assuming transfers are uniform within each quintile. The resulting `eta` gives the bottom 25% of lifetime earners 54% of transfers and the top 1% essentially none (0.2%), replacing the population-proportional default (25% and 1% respectively). The age profile of transfers within each group is unchanged. The program-level incidence and spending weights are packaged in `ogbra/data/transfer_incidence.csv`, and `tests/test_transfer_incidence.py` re-derives the `eta` group shares from that file.

A note on ranking concepts, because published incidence numbers for the same program differ sharply by how households are ranked. The World Bank's 2017 Public Expenditure Review ([Um Ajuste Justo](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/884871511196609355/pdf/121480-REVISED-PORTUGUESE-Brazil-Public-Expenditure-Review-Overview-Portuguese-Final-revised.pdf)) reports that only about 12% of BPC reaches the poorest quintile, with 43% going to the top two — but it ranks households by income *including* the benefit, and a benefit equal to a full minimum wage mechanically lifts recipients up the distribution. Ranked by market income — the concept behind BPC's means test (per capita income below a quarter of the minimum wage) and its CEQ concentration coefficient of −0.45 — BPC is strongly pro-poor, which is what our quintile shares reflect. The model's `eta` distributes transfers across *lifetime-ability* groups, for which the market-income ranking is the appropriate proxy. The same review confirms qualitatively that the abono salarial and unemployment insurance go mostly to better-off (formal) households, consistent with the moment-fitted shares we use for them.

## Public Pensions

Brazil's public pension system is the dominant item in the government budget: the general regime (RGPS) paid benefits of 8.1% of GDP in 2025, and total public pension spending — RGPS plus the civil-servant regimes (RPPS) at all levels of government, military pensions, and the BPC assistance benefit — reached 13.2% of GDP in 2022 ([IPEA](https://www.ipea.gov.br/portal/beneficiometro/beneficiometro-artigos/previdencia-social); [Senado RAF 110](https://www2.senado.leg.br/bdsf/bitstream/handle/id/863708/RAF110_MAR2026.pdf)).

`OG-BRA` models this with OG-Core's Defined Benefits pension system (`pension_system = "Defined Benefits"`), which matches the structure of the post-2019-reform RGPS benefit rule: a replacement rate applied to average career earnings, increasing with years of contribution (the statutory rule is 60% of the full-career average plus 2 percentage points per contribution year beyond 20 for men, from a minimum retirement age of 65). We set `retirement_age = 65`, `avg_earn_num_years = 45` (the reform bases benefits on the full contribution history), and `yr_contrib = 35` (a typical full contribution career, for which the statutory replacement rate is 90%).

The per-year accrual rate `alpha_db` is not set to its statutory value. The model has a single national formula and no informal sector, minimum-wage benefit floor, or benefit ceiling, so we anchor `alpha_db` to the aggregate instead: it is set to 0.0289 so that steady-state pension outlays equal 12.5% of GDP, within the range of Brazil's observed total public pension spending (the statutory accrual of about 0.026 produces 11.1% of GDP in the model). The employer payroll contribution is captured by `tau_payroll = 0.28` (see the taxes chapter).

Note: OG-Core's Defined Benefits implementation (through at least ogcore 0.16.3) has never been exercised by a country model and contains array/scalar bugs in its numba loops (`p.retire` passed as scalar, scalar `g_y` and steady-state `w` indexed as arrays); a fix is being submitted to OG-Core. Until it is released, running OG-BRA with the DB system requires the patched ogcore.
