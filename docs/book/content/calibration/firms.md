# Calibration of Firms Parameters

## Aggregate Production Function and Capital Accumulation

The [OG-Core firm theory documentation](https://pslmodels.github.io/OG-Core/content/theory/firms.html) outlines the constant returns to scale, constant elasticity of substitution production function of the representative firm.  This function has two parameters; the elasticity of substitution and capital's share of output.

### Elasticity of substitution

`OG-BRA`'s default parameterization has an elasticity of substitution of $\varepsilon=1.0$, which implies a Cobb-Douglas production function.

### Capital's share of output

Here, we use a default value of $\gamma =0.407$, which is derived from the ILO's SDG indicator 10.4.1 (labour share of GDP, series `SDG_1041_NOC_RT_A`) for Brazil.  Capital's share is computed as one minus the labour share.

## The Multi-Industry Calibration

`OG-BRA` includes a nine-industry ($M=9$, $I=7$ consumption goods) calibration built from the annual Brazilian input-output table estimated by [Alves-Passoni and Freitas (2023)](https://repositorio.ipea.gov.br/handle/11058/13345) from the IBGE Supply and Use Tables (67 activities, 126 products; the series reproduces the official IBGE benchmark matrix in 2015). The base year is 2018. Compact extracts of the table ship with the package in `ogbra/data` (see `data_prep/multi_industry` for the extraction script and provenance).

Because (re-)calibration is a rare event, the calibration is stored as a static parameter overlay, `ogbra_multisector_default_parameters.json`, which the model loads rather than recomputing at run time. The overlay is (re)generated by the tool `ogbra.create_multisector_calibration`, which assembles the construction functions in `ogbra.input_output` (`get_alpha_c`, `get_io_matrix`, `get_gamma`, `get_Z`, `get_employment`, `get_tau_c`, `get_cit_rate`), and `tests/test_input_output.py` keeps the committed JSON in sync with a fresh rebuild. The example `examples/run_og_bra_multi_industry.py` loads the overlay, solves the steady state, simulates the baseline transition path, and runs a small illustrative reform (a corporate income tax cut) for comparison, as the sibling multi-industry examples do.

### Industries and consumption goods

The nine industries, in the order the model uses them, are:

1. Agriculture, forestry & fishing
2. Mining & extractives
3. Electricity & gas
4. Water, sewage & waste
5. Construction
6. Trade, transport & accommodation
7. Finance, real estate & business services
8. Public, social & other services
9. Manufacturing

Manufacturing is kept **last** because OG-Core treats the final industry $M$ as the numeraire (all prices are relative to it) and the sole producer of investment goods. Electricity and water are broken out as their own industries — and their own consumption goods — rather than folded into utilities or services, so that energy and water can be tracked separately in the calibration. Real estate (imputed owner-occupier dwelling rent) is grouped with finance and business services — the standard FIRE aggregation, and the treatment OG-PHL also uses — rather than standing alone, because a sector that is almost pure capital return has no meaningful labor margin for the model's production function. The industries aggregate the table's 67 activities via `ogbra.constants.ACTIVITY_TO_SECTOR`. One caveat: CNAE groups electricity with piped gas and steam in a single division, so the "Electricity & gas" industry is an electricity-dominated bundle (piped-gas distribution is small in Brazil and district heating negligible); a pure-power split would require ANEEL/ABEGÁS revenue data.

The seven consumption goods, their expenditure shares $\alpha_i$ (household final consumption at consumer prices, `get_alpha_c`), and their effective consumption tax rates $\tau_i$ are:

| Consumption good | $\alpha_i$ | $\tau_i$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Food & beverages | 0.196 | 0.127 |
| Electricity | 0.027 | 0.340 |
| Water & sanitation | 0.009 | 0.045 |
| Fuels | 0.057 | 0.291 |
| Other non-durables | 0.120 | 0.229 |
| Durables | 0.101 | 0.227 |
| Services | 0.490 | 0.064 |

The per-good rate (`get_tau_c`) is taxes on products embedded in household consumption divided by consumption net of those taxes, from the use table's tax column. This is the same effective-rate concept as the single-industry `tau_c`, and its consumption-weighted average (0.127) reproduces the single rate — but the disaggregation makes Brazil's indirect-tax structure visible: electricity carries a 34% effective rate (chiefly ICMS, which many states levy at 25–30% on power) and fuels 29%, against 6% on services. Making the heavy taxation of electricity explicit is one of the practical payoffs of giving electricity its own consumption good.

### Private capital's share by industry

The table reports three factor rows: compensation of employees (labor), gross operating surplus (capital, including land rents), and gross mixed income — the earnings of the self-employed, which are part labor and part capital. Assigning all mixed income to capital would badly overstate capital's share where self-employment is common, so we split it by Gollin's second adjustment: the self-employed earn capital and labor in the same proportion as the incorporated part of the economy. With $\phi$ the economy-wide capital share of the non-mixed factor income,

$$\gamma_m = \frac{\text{operating surplus}_m + \phi\,\text{mixed income}_m}{\text{compensation}_m + \text{operating surplus}_m + \text{mixed income}_m}, \qquad \phi = \frac{\text{operating surplus}}{\text{compensation} + \text{operating surplus}} = 0.428,$$

so 0.428 is Brazil's own Gollin-corrected capital share from the same 2018 table. Because $\phi$ is the economy's own capital share, the industry shares already average (factor-income-weighted) to it; we apply a rounding-level rescale to hit 0.428 exactly, preserving the cross-industry pattern, then carve public capital's share $\gamma_{g,m}=0.05$ out of capital, not labor, leaving the private shares $\gamma_m$ below. (The single-industry default above uses the ILO labor-share series instead; the two sources differ by about two percentage points.)

Agriculture shows why the split matters. Its factor income is 19% compensation, 35% operating surplus, and **47% mixed income** — nearly five times the economy-wide share, because smallholder family farming is largely self-employed. Booking all of that as capital gives a 0.81 raw capital share, and the cruder alternative of rescaling that raw share to the aggregate still leaves 0.72; the Gollin split instead moves the smallholder-labor portion to the labor side, giving 0.55 — 0.50 after the public-capital carve-out (table below). What genuine capital intensity remains is agribusiness land rents and machinery inside operating surplus. Mining and electricity (0.77) are capital-heavy with little self-employment; the finance, real estate & business services aggregate (0.61) is pulled up by the near-pure-capital return to the dwelling stock it contains; public and social services (0.08) are labor almost entirely.

### The input-output matrix

OG-Core maps household demand for the $I$ consumption goods into demand for the $M$ industries' output through the `io_matrix` (the $\pi_{i,m}$ coefficients in the [OG-Core market-clearing documentation](https://pslmodels.github.io/OG-Core/content/theory/market_clearing.html)). Because OG-Core's production side has no intermediate inputs, the object needed is the **value added embodied in final consumption**: of one real spent on good $i$, how much value added accrues to each industry once the full domestic supply chain is traced. `ogbra.input_output.get_io_matrix` computes it as a standard Leontief value-added-content calculation, $v \circ \left(L\, D\, f_i\right)$, using the table's own market-share matrix $D$ and domestic Leontief inverse $L$, with imported content excluded and each row normalized to sum to one.

The supply-chain trace matters. A naive direct-use attribution (allocating each consumed product to whichever industry supplies it, with no trace) books 73% of food spending to Manufacturing — because households buy *processed* food — and 99.8% of electricity spending to the electricity industry. Traced through the supply chain, food value added splits 31% agriculture / 28% manufacturing / 22% trade & transport, and the electricity good is 72% electricity-industry value added with the rest in the services that produce and deliver it:

| Good | Attribution | Agriculture | Trade & transport | Finance, RE & business | Manufacturing | Electricity & gas |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Food & beverages | direct use | 0.22 | 0.06 | 0.00 | 0.73 | — |
| Food & beverages | value-added content | 0.31 | 0.22 | 0.14 | 0.28 | — |
| Electricity | direct use | — | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 1.00 |
| Electricity | value-added content | — | 0.06 | 0.13 | 0.05 | 0.72 |

### Sector total factor productivity

No published series of Brazilian sector-TFP *levels* matches OG-Core's technology, so `OG-BRA` constructs $Z_m$ directly as the Solow residual of the model's own production function,

```{math}
:label: EqZmSolowBRA
  Z_m = \frac{Y_m}{K_m^{\gamma_m}\,K_{g}^{\gamma_{g,m}}\,L_m^{1-\gamma_m-\gamma_{g,m}}},
```

normalized so Manufacturing has $Z_m=1$ (public capital is one common stock, so its term cancels under the normalization). $Y_m$ is industry value added from the table; $L_m$ is the table's occupations row (104.3 million jobs in 2018), a physical head count measured separately from the factor payments; $K_m$ allocates the national capital stock across industries by capital-income share — the distribution implied by capital mobility at a common return — with the stock anchored to the Penn World Table capital-output ratio for Brazil (4.36 in 2018).

The resulting capital shares and productivities:

| Industry | $\gamma_m$ | $Z_m$ | Effective CIT |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Agriculture, forestry & fishing | 0.497 | 0.512 | 0.013 |
| Mining & extractives | 0.771 | 1.495 | 0.039 |
| Electricity & gas | 0.768 | 1.528 | 0.104 |
| Water, sewage & waste | 0.465 | 1.053 | 0.125 |
| Construction | 0.351 | 0.548 | 0.089 |
| Trade, transport & accommodation | 0.346 | 0.709 | 0.099 |
| Finance, real estate & business services | 0.606 | 1.450 | 0.082 |
| Public, social & other services | 0.075 | 0.596 | 0.057 |
| Manufacturing | 0.330 | 1.000 | 0.141 |

The pattern is the familiar Brazilian one: highly productive, capital-intensive extractives and utilities; a large finance-and-real-estate services sector whose capital intensity reflects the dwelling stock it embeds; labor-intensive agriculture, construction, and public services, which are also the least productive per unit of measured factor input.

### Per-industry corporate tax rates

The last column above is the effective corporate income tax rate by industry (`get_cit_rate`): IRPJ plus CSLL collections by CNAE section from the Receita Federal's arrecadação data (averaged over 2017–2019, packaged as `ogbra/data/rfb_cit_by_section.csv`), divided by the industry's gross operating surplus from the input-output table. Effective rates average about 9% — far below the 34% statutory rate — because of the presumed-profit and Simples regimes, sector exemptions, and the tax base being narrower than gross operating surplus. Agriculture pays the least (special regimes, and a surplus that is largely land rent rather than taxable corporate profit) and manufacturing the most. The finance, real estate & business services aggregate carries a low effective rate because its operating surplus includes imputed owner-occupier dwelling rent, which is not corporate profit and dilutes the denominator. We calibrate to the effective rates because the model applies `cit_rate` to the whole of capital income; a user who wants statutory incidence can override the overlay.

### Funded public capital

The overlay keeps $\gamma_{g,m}=0.05$ for every industry, and — unlike the single-industry default it overlays — funds it: `alpha_I = 0.02` (public investment share of GDP, from the IMF Public Investment Management Assessment data for Brazil), `initial_Kg_ratio = 0.35`, and `delta_g_annual = 0.047` (the depreciation rate implied by the steady-state identity $K_g/Y = \alpha_I/(g+\delta_g)$). This is not optional: with $\gamma_g>0$ but no public investment, the steady-state public capital stock is zero and the production side is inconsistent — the last industry's resource constraint cannot clear.

### Converting the utility weights to the multi-good consumption units

OG-Core prices the composite consumption bundle as $\tilde{p} = \prod_i \left((1+\tau_i)p_i/\alpha_i\right)^{\alpha_i}$; dividing each price by a fractional budget share inflates the index by the constant $k = \prod_i \alpha_i^{-\alpha_i}$, which depends only on the shares. The seven Brazilian shares give $k = 4.30$: the same basket carries a price about four times higher, and a measured composite quantity about four times smaller, than in the single-good model. Two preference parameters are defined relative to consumption in those units — the disutility-of-labor weights $\chi_n$ and the bequest weight $\chi_b$ — so the overlay rescales both by $k^{\sigma-1} = 4.30^{0.5} \approx 2.07$ (at $\sigma=1.5$). The factor is derived from the budget shares and the utility curvature, not fitted, and it restores the household first-order conditions exactly (see the equivalence test below). Without it, the multi-industry model quietly solves a *different economy* — households work more and save less because consumption looks artificially cheap in utility terms.

## Solving the Multi-Industry Steady State

With heterogeneous capital shares and TFP, the calibrated equilibrium relative prices $p_m$ span 0.13 (mining) to 2.09 (public services). OG-Core seeds every price at $p_m=1$ and never adjusts the price guesses directly, so a direct solve diverges. `OG-BRA` follows OG-PHL and solves by *continuation* (homotopy), in `solve_ss_by_continuation`:

1. Solve a **homogeneous anchor economy** with the flat capital share $\gamma=0.378$ ($=0.428-\gamma_g$) and $Z=1$ in every industry. There all equilibrium prices genuinely equal one, matching OG-Core's seed, and it solves immediately.
2. Blend the anchor toward the calibrated economy with a dial $t\in[0,1]$: $\gamma(t)=(1-t)\gamma_{\text{flat}}+t\gamma_m$ and $Z(t)=(1-t)+tZ_m$, morphing shares and TFP together.
3. Walk $t$ from 0 to 1 in adaptive steps, each solved as a non-baseline run warm-started from the previous step (this is how prices are carried forward). The step grows after a success and halves after a failure.

At $t=1$ the blended parameters are the calibrated parameters, so that solution **is** the calibrated steady state (the full walk takes about two minutes). The baseline transition path reuses this converged steady state rather than re-solving from a cold start; a reform scenario re-solves its own steady state warm-started off the baseline, which converges because it is only a small policy perturbation. The transition path uses damping $\nu=0.2$ (the OG-Core default 0.4 oscillates in the analogous OG-PHL configuration).

## Interpreting the Multi-Industry Steady State

Three features of the converged steady state are structural properties of OG-Core's accounting, not artifacts of the Brazilian calibration:

* **Manufacturing's nominal output share (~65%) is mechanically inflated.** OG-Core routes all non-consumption final demand — investment, government purchases — through the single numeraire industry, so its share far exceeds Manufacturing's ~12% value-added share in the data. The data-comparable object is the consumption-driven composition: model labor allocations correlate 0.86 with the table's industry labor income (compensation plus the labor part of mixed income) once the numeraire is excluded, with the remaining gaps concentrated exactly where they must be — public services and construction, whose real-world demand is government consumption and investment.
* **Comparisons with data employment must be in efficiency units.** The model has a single economy-wide wage, so its $L_m$ is labor *income* at a common rate; comparing it to head counts misreads sectors with far-below-average wages (agriculture has 12.8% of Brazil's jobs but 4.2% of labor income).
* **With $\gamma_g > 0$, industry disaggregation itself changes the equilibrium.** Public capital is a common, nonrival input, so private factors face decreasing returns and marginal cost rises with industry scale: even *literally identical* industries do not price at 1 — the small ones price below the numeraire by a factor $\left(Y_M/Y_m\right)^{-\gamma_g/(1-\gamma_g)}$, and the numeraire is much larger because it absorbs all non-consumption final demand. Exact $M$-invariance is therefore only defined at $\gamma_g=0$, and there `tests/test_multisector_equivalence.py` verifies it: with identical technologies and the utility-weight conversion above, $M=9, I=7$ reproduces the $M=1$ economy's $r$, $w$, $K/Y$, $L$ and nominal consumption to twelve digits. That test is what certifies the io_matrix bookkeeping and the units conversion as exactly neutral.
